Alt text for Journal of Applied Physics submission: Oxidation-budgeted design of lead-free RbGeI3 solar cells

Figure 1: Schematic of the five-layer planar stack, with arrows tracing electron flow toward the front electrode and hole flow toward the back contact under simulated sunlight.
Figure 2: Four-panel line plot of efficiency, open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, and fill factor against bulk defect density. Efficiency peaks at the lowest density and collapses at high densities, driven mainly by falling open-circuit voltage.
Figure 3: Band-edge diagram plotting the conduction and valence band edges with split electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels under illumination. It is annotated with the electron-blocking offset at the back interface and the small extraction step at the front interface.
Figure 4: Rectangular current-voltage curve with a dashed output-power curve peaking at the marked maximum-power point near 0.96 volts. The curve is sharply rectangular, with no visible s-kink or rollover.
Figure 5: Four histograms from 200 randomized Latin-hypercube runs showing efficiency clustered near 25 percent with a standard deviation of 1.8 percentage points. Voltage, current, and fill factor show similarly tight distributions under the same parameter sampling.
Figure 6: Four-panel line plot showing open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current density, fill factor, and efficiency between 280 and 400 kelvin. All four quantities decline as the temperature rises.
Figure 7: Optimization trace in which the per-generation best efficiency fluctuates while the best-so-far curve climbs stepwise from about 27.6 to 28.3 percent across six generations of a six-parameter genetic-algorithm search.
Figure 8: Tolerance plot showing efficiency falling steeply as bulk or rear-interface defect density increases. Short-circuit current stays nearly flat while open-circuit voltage and fill factor both collapse as the defect levels rise.
Figure 9: Stepwise loss chart descending from the 27 percent ideal point through optical and defect penalties to about 21 percent. A higher branch shows the efficiency recovered when the back-surface field is added.
Figure 10: Efficiency versus defect energy level, forming a shallow symmetric minimum at mid-gap and recovering toward both band edges. The minimum is the expected signature of mid-gap states being the most lethal recombination centers.
Figure 11: Efficiency versus electron and hole capture cross-sections, showing symmetric large traps as the worst case. Efficiency recovers to nearly the baseline value when electron capture is small, whatever the hole cross-section.
Figure 12: Efficiency versus background acceptor density, peaking near 3 times 10 to the 15th per cubic centimeter and declining gradually at higher doping. The penalty stays modest across most of the swept range.
Figure 13: Efficiency and fill factor versus series resistance, both falling steadily as resistance rises from 0 to 8 ohm square centimeters. The curves are computed on the stacked realistic point rather than the ideal device.
Figure 14: Efficiency versus absorber mobility on a logarithmic axis, rising nearly linearly from about 18 percent at low mobility to about 24 percent at high mobility. Electron and hole mobilities are set equal.
Figure 15: Open-circuit voltage versus temperature extrapolated to zero kelvin, giving 1.48 electron volts. That value lies slightly above the bandgap, which points to an extrapolation artifact rather than a bulk-recombination signature.
Figure 16: Grouped comparison of RbGeI3 and CsGeI3 efficiency at ideal, realistic, and high-oxidation defect budgets. The rubidium device leads at every level while the cesium open-circuit voltage stays pinned.
Figure 17: Ladder chart of efficiency under combined series and shunt resistance penalties, with a dashed marker at the 20 percent threshold. Successive rungs add a further parasitic resistance penalty to the stacked realistic device.
Figure 18: Color grid of back-surface-field efficiency over doping and thickness, plus a side panel showing that the field halves the efficiency cost of rear-interface traps. Doping forms the grid rows and thickness the columns.
Figure 19: Depth profile of the photogeneration rate decaying exponentially from the illuminated front surface through the 700 nanometer absorber layer. The curve uses a square-root absorption law normalized to the incident spectrum.
Figure 20: Parity scatter plot of machine-learning predicted versus simulated efficiency, clustering tightly along the diagonal. The held-out twenty-percent test set gives a coefficient of determination of 0.907.
Figure 21: Horizontal bar ranking of parameter importance from the held-out surrogate, with bulk defect density first, followed by bandgap and electron mobility. Band-edge densities of states rank last.
Figure 22: Overlay of simulated and digitized measured current-voltage curves for a control and a passivated device, matching closely except near the knee of the control curve.
Figure 23: Histograms and scatter plots placing the realistic efficiency window above the maximum of a frozen inorganic database snapshot, with calibration markers shown for scale. The snapshot is fixed to entries published before 2021.
Figure 24: Efficiency versus germanium-vacancy formation energy, mapping processing conditions onto rungs of the defect ladder from glovebox through ambient to the metallic limit. Dots give the equilibrium mapping and shaded regions the processing-condition bands.

Table I: Screening matrix listing the eight combinations of two electron and four hole transport layers around the common absorber, all simulated at the same absorber thickness for a fair comparison.
Table II: Performance table of voltage, current, fill factor, and efficiency for all eight screened architectures. The nickel-oxide device leads at this thickness, and the titanium-oxide and copper-iodide stack was selected for the optimization.
Table III: Optimization ledger giving each swept parameter range, the adopted value, and the acceptance rule behind it. Eleven parameters were swept in sequence, with full traces in the supplementary material.
Table IV: Layer-by-layer parameter table of thickness, bandgap, affinity, permittivity, densities of states, mobilities, doping, and defect densities for the final device. Transport-layer bulk defect densities were carried over from the initial deck.
Table V: Comparison of the adopted reference device against the genetic-algorithm best-found point, plus sensitivity rows for densities of states and realistic optics. The best-found point is reported as optimizer reach rather than as an admissible device.
Table VI: Benchmark table of reported lead-free cell efficiencies from the literature alongside the proposed device. All comparator values were verified against primary sources, and all entries are simulation studies.
Table VII: Gap-control table of RbGeI3 versus CsGeI3 voltage, current, and efficiency at ideal, realistic, and high-oxidation defect budgets. Only the absorber bandgap differs, so this isolates a gap effect rather than cation chemistry.
Table VIII: Calibration table of measured versus rebuilt control and champion devices at 318 kelvin, agreeing within 2 percent on every metric. Fitted parameters are defect densities plus series resistance on the control.
